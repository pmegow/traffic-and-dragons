The design under review (the brief every lens read)

THE VILLAGE — design under panel review (TODO #6, owner design session 2026-09-12)

The owner's framing (verbatim intent)

  • A single-location campaign.
  • No quests, no dangers.
  • Populated entirely by the player's characters from the character library (every saved character is a resident).
  • Each character has their own quarters — a house, a manor, a hut.
  • A player may leave an item in their quarters and it must stay there until they retrieve it. A robust location-based inventory retention system is required.
  • There is a tavern, a blacksmith, a trading post, an alchemist, guild halls, a healer, an animal handler.
  • Places where a player can empty their inventory and spend gold. Items a player SELLS at a shop last the session but are not granted permanence (unlike items left in a house).
  • The open question the owner raised: how does the player specify WHICH character they experience the village through? "Would a simple 'load character' work? Seems like it SHOULD."

The engine-side answer proposed to the owner (Fable, 2026-09-12)

  1. The village is its own campaign, not an attachment. One persistent campaign in the campaign picker, created from a shipped village blueprint, kind: "village". Characters move between it and adventure campaigns through the character library (which already exists: server-synced, per-account). The August-2026 framing (a "mini-skeleton" that attaches to other campaigns at their seams) is deferred to phase 2 as optional departure/return beats.
  2. Which character you play = the existing hero swap. Every library character enters the village as a resident NPC with a full character sheet (the existing library-import-as-companion path). "Load character" is a picker over the residents that calls the existing _switchPlayerCharacter: it promotes a resident to the hero slot and demotes the previous hero back to a resident. So a new character walks past a retired character's door because the retired character is standing in it.
  3. The library stays the source of truth. The village holds copies. Switching away from a character, or leaving the village for an adventure, writes the sheet back to the library. (Otherwise a sword bought in the village would be missing when a new campaign is started from the library.)
  4. Item retention rides the existing map graph. Every map node already carries an items list and the GM already files [LOCATION_ITEM:name|placed] / taken. Because the village campaign never ends, a house node keeps its items across sessions for free. Additions: (a) a village-kind gate refuses taken unless the hero is the taker (the GM may not invent theft or loss in the village); (b) ownership on house nodes so the HOME block injected on entry lists "your stuff" authoritatively; (c) shop-sold items become that shop's WARES with a turn stamp (the existing wares/hours mechanism) and roll off at a session boundary — proposed boundary: when the hero leaves the village. They never touch node items, which is the permanence distinction.
  5. The mute list — the one real engine change. Every GM turn today gets prompt blocks and engine notes whose job is to push the story forward. With kind: "village" these are switched off at ONE boundary (a field on the note registry entry + the prompt assembly reading the kind once), never by per-site checks: the DRIVE rule and its three-step telegraph; the deadline/commitment observers; agenda births and beats; whispers (rumours); the money-at-stake note; quest-stale, arc-staging, arc-drift and expired-thread nudges; the reckless wildcard and the montage on the fourth button; the combat tracker and death propagation (combat tags refused with an honest reason). What stays on: presence and mood audits, relationship axes, location filing and location items, the clock, wares and hours, the character sheet, rest.
  6. The Village Hall (owner: "A+ idea", 2026-08-24) — finished campaigns are deposited as physical mementos on the hall's walls (a mural, a mounted map, a crown on a plinth), fed by the story-compiler output. Same village store, zero new mechanics. Sequenced after the story compiler (#219 stage 2).

Three forks put to the owner

  1. Village as its own campaign (recommended) vs the attached mini-skeleton.
  2. Library as source of truth with write-back on every switch and departure (recommended) vs the village as truth.
  3. What "session" means for shop wares: until the hero leaves the village (recommended) vs until a long rest.

Product context the panel should hold

  • Solo player, a party of model-narrated companions; the GM is a language model narrating in second person; state lives in tags the model emits and the engine parses (the "anti-drift" stack is the product's validated core).
  • The character library is server-synced and per account; "companion import" already carries cross-campaign relationship history, by owner decree.
  • The game already has: a campaign clock, location hours, a wares economy (shops file wares with prices), rest, relationship axes between characters, presence audits, a fourth engine-authored suggestion button (rest / use consumable / accept offer / buy / montage / reckless wildcard), and a denouement written at a campaign's end.
  • Owner rules that bind: no ledgers as a story device (paper-trail plots are banned in fiction); prose length is not a positive; no silent failures; one uniform registry/table per family of variants (no conditionals sprinkled through call sites); never manufacture a companion "want" — only lived history births one.

Synthesis

Written by Fable after reading all nine reviews. The lenses agree on far more than they disagree, and most of what they agree on changes the proposal.

The verdict in one line

Every lens endorses the village as a place and as its own campaign. Every lens that looked at the mute list says the same thing in its own vocabulary: a list of things switched off is not a design. The proposal must say what a village turn is for, and it must switch on the residents' initiative while it switches off danger.

Where nine lenses converge
  1. Substitute, never subtract (Le Guin A, Jemisin B, Laws B, Baker A, Abercrombie F2, Leiber 1, Harper F2, Moran D). Muting the DRIVE rule leaves its registry slot empty, and the surviving STYLE clause "when nothing much has changed, write LESS" then applies to every turn forever. The village kind needs a DRIVE replacement in the same slot — a scene must offer a person, a change, or a cost in coin or time, and end on motion, never on danger — and a coverage guard that the slot is never empty. Test: twenty village turns; count GM-invented threats and turns under a word floor.
  2. Cut on danger, not on initiative (Harper F2, Baker B, Laws A and C, Leiber 3, Abercrombie F3). Agenda beats, whispers and companion initiative are the only instruments that let a resident do something unprompted, and a village of retired heroes is the first place where their preconditions are reliably met. Keep them on, re-aimed: whispers become residents talking about each other's finished campaigns; the owner's "only lived history births a want" rule is satisfied in its purest form by a resident carrying three hundred turns of record. Mute only DRIVE, the telegraph, the deadline and commitment observers, and combat.
  3. The swap is the main verb and it is built as a rare one (Laws D, Le Guin D, Baker E, Harper F6, Jemisin D, Moran E). Three code facts: the library import is gated at the party cap of four, so "every saved character is a resident" collides with it on the fifth; each swap demotes the outgoing hero to a party member, so the party fills with everyone you have ever played; and a swap costs a billed GM turn plus two turns of POV correction. Residency must be a non-party state with a sheet; the swap promotes from residency and demotes to residency; in the village the handoff beat is the encounter with the character you were, not the resumption; and the swap should not spend a GM turn. Test: three swaps in ten turns with the person-drift check.
  4. Item permanence: the code does not yet keep the promise (Jemisin C, Baker D, Harper F5, Leiber 4, Moran C, Abercrombie F4, Gygax B). Location items are name-keyed with no quantity, so two potions collapse to one and a second "iron sword" un-takes the first; items file to the node the hero stands in, not the house the prose names; a placed item at a missing node vanishes with no toast; there is no owner field; the player-pickup path through [ITEM_GAINED:] bypasses the tag the gate would refuse; and shop wares key to the settlement node with a cap of two to six and silent eviction of the oldest. Every one of these is a failing test to write before any village code.
  5. The HOME block must not be a ledger read aloud (Le Guin C, Abercrombie F4). The list is data for the panel; the prose names two or three objects by what happened to them. Stamp placed items with the turn and provenance so there is something to say.
  6. The fourth button collapses to buy, rest or nothing (Harper F4, Laws C, Moran B). Give the village its own rung in the same ladder — call on a resident, ask what they have been doing, a round at the tavern — pinned by a test on a full-HP, zero-gold, zero-quest state.
  7. Something must change between visits (Jemisin E, Gygax C). Wares thinned, an hour shifted, a relationship axis moved. A village that is identical on return is restoration, which one lens names as the genre's failure mode.
  8. The Hall belongs in v1, degraded (Abercrombie F1, Le Guin E, Gygax F). Chapters and defining moments already exist without the story compiler; each door is a campaign that happened. And every memento must carry one thing the campaign never resolved, which the denouement already names.
  9. The economy needs supply, not risk (Gygax A). The existing wares cap and seven-day restock are the constraint; do not treat the village as an unlimited counter.
  10. Rewards and tempo (Moran A, D). Either a small village XP list, rate-limited, or an explicit "the village pays nothing" stated in the fiction. The quest log may hold village goals the DRIVE rule cannot touch; that is a quiet stake, not a ledger.
  11. The registry home already exists (Baker C). The note-shapes table carries a combat: axis; the village is the second mode on that axis, not a new field beside it.
  12. Write the throughline first (Laws F). One sentence of core activity, and re-derive the mute list from it. The sentence the reviews converge on is meet the people you used to be.
Where they split
  • The montage. Harper: keep it, retuned to a domestic register. Moran: muting is correct because the village is permanently "due". Laws: measure before deciding. Ruling: off in v1, and log how often it would have fired; a domestic variant is a phase-2 question.
  • Whether a place with no cost has weight. Abercrombie and Leiber raise it and concede it to the owner's premise; Baker argues from a stated taste and says so. Not a design change; the no-danger premise stands.
The three forks, settled by the panel
  1. Own campaign. Unanimous among the lenses that took a position (eight of nine).
  2. Library as source of truth. No lens dissents; two add the same qualifier: one writer, and the sheet the prompt reads must be the sheet the player edits. Jemisin asks that the village keep its own memory of a resident apart from the record, which the NPC memory tier already does.
  3. Wares boundary: neither option. Gygax and Jemisin both point out the campaign clock already expires wares at seven game days; "when the hero leaves" and "at a long rest" each invent a second time unit for a fact the clock owns. Ruling: the clock.
What changes in the design before build

The brief's five points survive; point five is rewritten from a mute list into a mode: one replacement DRIVE clause, initiative instruments on, danger instruments off, one registry axis. Two new points enter: residency as a non-party state with a swap that costs nothing, and a pre-build test list for item permanence. The Hall moves from phase 2 to a degraded v1. The Harper lens rubric

This is a rubric of John Harper's documented positions, not the man. Every position below is cited by entry number and confidence grade; ○ entries inform questions only.

1. Verdict

This lens reads the village as a downtime phase given a place — the "different toolbox" of §6.1 (●) built as its own room rather than as a mode inside the action toolbox — and the proposal serves that purpose well at the structural level while leaving the two things that make Harper's downtime work, a world with its own agenda (6.5 ●) and a cheaper price per unit of quiet (6.2 ●), unaddressed.

2. Findings

F1 — The village is the product's strongest agreement with this lens; say so out loud. Positions: 6.1 (●), 7.2 (●), 12 ▸ Downtime weighting (●). The proposal separates the quiet phase into its own campaign kind with its own rule set. §12 grades the phase separation as the durable Harper position and the dice inside it as always negotiable — so a diceless, dangerless downtime space is not a deviation, it is the part he kept. It also dissolves this lens's largest standing dissent (§10, pre-written structure vs. play-to-find-out): the village has no skeleton, no act goals, no ending button. What the player experiences is the one surface in this product where nothing is steering. Recommendation: keep as is, and state the no-spine property in the brief as an intentional design promise, not an absence.

F2 — The mute list switches off the world's agenda along with its threats. Positions: 6.5 (●), 7.1 (●), 2.5 (●). Item 5 mutes agenda births and beats, whispers, deadlines and the DRIVE rule in one gesture. 6.5's entanglements position is that the world acts on the player from its own agenda precisely during downtime, and 7.1 makes "a concrete desire and preferred method" the GM's first job for every NPC. The proposal keeps mood and presence audits — which describe residents — and removes the only instruments that let them do anything. Concretely, the player walks a street of twenty of their own characters, each of whom stands still until spoken to. The boundary the mute list should cut on is danger, not initiative. Recommendation: keep agenda beats and whispers on for kind:"village"; mute DRIVE, telegraph, combat, deadline/commitment observers. Test: 15 village turns with no player-initiated contact — count turns in which a resident does something unprompted.

F3 — One toolbox, one price, and the montage is muted exactly where the quiet is longest. Positions: 6.1 (●), 6.2 (●), §10 ▸ "Downtime at score prices". 6.2 frames downtime as cheap in table energy. This product bills by the turn, so a village turn costs what a combat turn costs; §10 already names that as the real economic divergence, and #308's montage as the product agreeing with the lens. The proposal mutes the montage. montageDue() (game.js:200) fires on stretches with no combat, move or rest tag — which is the definition of a village session, so the instrument is firing correctly and being switched off at the one place it was built for. Recommendation: keep the montage in the village (retune its copy to a domestic register); mute the reckless wildcard only.

F4 — The engine's fourth button collapses to a shopping button. (Code-grounded.) Positions: 7.9 (●), 9.3 (●), 7.5 (●). engineFourthAction() (game.js:168-186) is an ordered ladder: rest-when-hurt → use-consumable-that-answers-a-need → accept-an-offered-quest → buy-live-wares → montage → wildcard. In a village with no quests, no danger and a healed hero, rungs 1–3 never fire and 5–6 are muted, so the button is "Buy the …" or null. 7.9 calls the suggestion surface the GM's instrument for pointing at the interesting part; here the engine points at the shop or says nothing. Recommendation: give kind:"village" its own ladder entry in the same registry — the highest-value rung this lens can name is 9.3's honest question, in-fiction ("Ask Ammut what she has been doing"), which the file grades the strongest scene tech in the corpus.

F5 — Item permanence makes the authority split load-bearing, and the code loses it silently. (Code-grounded.) Position: 7.8 (●) — authority split by category, and written down. fileLocationItem and autoTakeLocationItem (memory.js:393-411) both resolve currentNodeKey(), match items case-insensitively, and return silently when the node is missing or the name does not match: a taken for an unknown item is a no-op, and a placed filed before the house node exists vanishes with no toast and no console line. The village's whole premise is "leave it and it stays," so the arbiter of where the sword is must be written down — engine node list, or model prose. Today prose wins or loses invisibly, against the owner's no-silent-failures rule. Recommendation: before any village work, make both functions report; add a failing test that files [LOCATION_ITEM:X|placed] at a node that does not exist and asserts a surfaced error.

F6 — "Load character" costs a full model turn and two degraded ones. (Code-grounded.) Positions: 5.5 (●), 12 ▸ Direction of travel (●). _switchPlayerCharacter (ui-sheets.js:663-700) ends in sendAction(handoff,{silent:true}) and sets recentSwitch, which re-injects a POV block for roughly two turns. That machinery exists because a mid-adventure swap fights sessionLog momentum. In a village designed for walking a roster, each swap is a billed turn plus two turns of prompt overhead — the opposite of 5.5's stated speed-of-entry goal. Recommendation: for kind:"village", swap without a GM turn (re-render, POV block on the next player-initiated turn only), and measure turns-to-first-meaningful-action on entry.

3. On the three forks

  • Fork 1 (own campaign vs. attached mini-skeleton): own campaign. §12's downtime row grades phase separation as the durable position (●); an attachment re-merges the toolboxes 6.1 (●) keeps apart.
  • Fork 2 (library as truth vs. village as truth): this lens has no position — nothing in the evidence base speaks to persistence ownership. Its only angle is F6's turn cost at the boundary.
  • Fork 3 (wares roll off on leaving vs. on a long rest): on leaving. 4.5 (●) refreshes spendable resources at the phase boundary, and departure is the phase boundary; a long rest sits inside the phase.

4. Gaps

Four things this lens cannot judge. (a) The Blades GM chapter is unread (caution 1) — every GM principle above comes from Basics or Lasers & Feelings, so "how should a GM run a quiet scene" is answered from the thinnest part of the evidence. (b) Deep Cuts' diceless downtime is paywalled and not in the lens (§13) — the single most on-point Harper artifact for a no-dice, no-danger phase is exactly the one unavailable. (c) Blades is a crew game (caution 2): heat, turf and the faction ladder are the machinery that gives its downtime stakes, and none of it ports to one hero among their own retired characters, so the village's social half has no analog here. (d) No statement about AI narration, solo play, or persistence exists in this base (§13) — item retention, library write-back, and the Village Hall mementos are judged only through F5's authority-split question, not on their merits.

The Baker lens rubric

This review applies the Baker lens: a rubric of documented, published craft positions, not the person. Every position is cited by entry number and confidence grade (● primary, ◐ secondary).

1. Verdict

The Baker lens reads THE VILLAGE as a place where the fiction's standing attachments — a retired hero, a house, a kept sword — become addressable objects (1.1 ●, 7.2 ●), and the proposal serves that purpose well; where it does not serve it is that the mute list removes every surface capable of producing an outcome, leaving a world that is real (1.1) but in which nothing can happen (1.2 ●, 8.1 ●).

2. Findings

A. The mute list turns off the pushing and leaves only the recording. Positions: 7.3 ●, 8.1 ●, 8.3 ●, 1.2 ●. Item 5 mutes DRIVE (data.js:108, rule 3 of DEFAULT_RULES), the telegraph, deadline/commitment observers, agenda births and beats, whispers, the money note, the wildcard and the montage. What survives — presence audits, mood audits, location filing, location items, the clock, wares, hours, rest — is, without exception, a recording surface. 7.3 ● asks of every mechanism "does it push a scene, or only record one?"; after the mute, the answer for the entire village is "record". 8.1 ● asks what can produce an outcome neither the player nor the GM would have chosen: in the village as specified, nothing can. The player's experience is turns that cannot go wrong, which after a few visits is the same signal as turns that cannot go right (3.6 ● — intermittent is the load-bearing word). Recommendation: do not add danger (the owner ruled it out, and 0.6 warns this lens argues from a taste the owner may decline). Instead adopt 1.2's own measure as a test: instrument how many consecutive village turns pass with nothing happening to the character, and treat a long run as a defect rather than as success.

B. The residents are the proposal's best idea, and item 5 disarms them. Positions: 5.1 ●, 6.2 ●, 3.2 ●. Every resident enters with a full sheet, and companion imports carry cross-campaign relationship history by owner decree. Muting agenda births removes the only surface that mints a standing want; api.js:606 (buildAgendaOfferNote) is explicit that the GM must "never invent a want that is not on their sheet". So in the village, ordinary residents hold a status mood and nothing more — a reaction, not an engine (5.1 ●). 6.2 ● then becomes unavailable: with no impulses, the GM has nothing to defer decisions to, and falls back on whim (6.1 ●), which is the failure mode this whole family of tools exists to prevent. Recommendation: keep agenda births ON in the village. The owner rule "only lived history births a want" is satisfied, not violated, by a resident carrying three hundred turns of recorded history — that is the lived-history case in its purest form.

C. The mute flag has a home already; a new parallel field would be the second axis. Positions: 5.2 ●. NOTE_BUILDERS (api.js:1892) is a flat array of function references with no per-entry fields, so "a field on the note registry entry" cannot land there. The per-note metadata lives in the keyed map NOTE_SHAPES (api.js:1901), whose rows already carry a mode axis: combat:"fires" / combat:"silent". The village is the second instance of that class. 5.2 ● (kinds supply behaviour through a table) and the project's own abstraction rule both point the same way. Recommendation: widen the existing combat: field into a mode axis (modes:{combat:…, village:…}) rather than adding a second, independently-maintained mute list.

D. Item retention files to where the hero stands, not to the house named. Positions: 7.2 ●, 7.4 ●, 3.5 ●. fileLocationItem (memory.js:393–404) resolves its target as locResolve(currentNodeKey()) and writes into that node's items. If the GM narrates "you leave the sword in your quarters" while the party is standing in the tavern, the sword files to the tavern. In an adventure this is a rare misfile; in a village whose entire point is per-house permanence, it is the central promise failing silently (7.4 ● — what does the engine fall back to when the tag lands wrong?). Recommendation (test-first): a scripted case that emits [LOCATION_ITEM:x|placed] on a turn whose narration names a different node, and asserts which key received it — before any village work. Separately, note that the proposed gate (refuse taken unless the hero is the taker) is a deliberate removal of 3.5's crosshairs: nothing in the village can ever be taken. That is a defensible owner choice; 8.3 ● only asks that it be named as one.

E. The character swap becomes the primary interaction, and it is built as a rare one. Positions: 3.3 ●, 7.1 ●. _switchPlayerCharacter (ui-sheets.js:663) sets worldState.recentSwitch precisely because "the sessionLog is full of the OLD character as 'you'", and buildSysPrompt then spends two turns forcing the POV back. In an adventure a swap happens once in a while; in the village it is the main verb. Three swaps inside ten turns leaves a transcript braided out of three different "you"s, and the picker itself is the meta-surface 3.3 ● names as the slip point. Recommendation: scripted three-swap-in-ten-turns run with a person-drift check; and prefer a fictional swap trigger (you wake in your own bed) over a menu — 7.1 ● says the structure of who speaks, in what order, is the thing to audit.

3. On the three forks

  1. Own campaign vs attached mini-skeleton — own campaign. 7.5 ● says whatever decides what happened is your system, so choose it deliberately; the attached form puts a skeleton's authority and a mute list in the same turn, with no stated rule for which wins (2.1 ●'s unanswered question).
  2. Library as source of truth — this lens has no craft position on the storage question; it asks only that the arbiter be named and single (7.5 ●), which the recommended option does.
  3. Wares session boundary — weak lean to long rest, on 6.4 ● alone: rest is a repeating, player-visible tick that can be felt as a clock, while "when the hero leaves" fires once and can never be perceived as a countdown.

4. Gaps

The lens cannot judge whether a no-stakes village is good: §9 recuses it from prose and tone, and 0.6 records that where it argues for cost it argues from a stated taste the owner may decline outright. It has no position on an LLM acting as GM (§10) — nothing here about the model's reliability is Baker-sourced. Caution 0.2 blocks four of the eleven principles and one disclaim-tool (they assume other players at a table), which removes most of what the lens would otherwise say about a social hub. §11 records that the AW MC chapter's discussion of when to make a move was never read — exactly the question a deliberately quiet mode raises. And it has no evidence at all on the Village Hall mementos (item 6), the store/sync design, or the economy's numbers.

The Gygax lens rubric

This is a rubric of Gary Gygax's documented positions, not the man; every position below is cited by lens entry and confidence grade (● primary · ◐ secondary · ○ uncertain).

1. Verdict

This lens reads the village as the persistent home node of a campaign with no planned end (1.4 ◐) whose job is to make places and people durable between adventures (7.2 ◐) — and the proposal serves that well, except where it makes a zero-risk location the game's main channel for gold and goods (2.3 ●, 3.1 ●).

2. Findings

A. The riskless economy is the one place this lens objects on its own terms. Positions: 3.1 ● (scarcity; Monty Haul is a named failure mode), 2.3 ● (treasure's worth scales with the risk taken to win it), 7.3 ● (not a simulation). The proposal makes the village the sole place with a blacksmith, trading post, alchemist and healer, explicitly "where a player can empty their inventory and spend gold," with no dangers. Every acquisition in the village is bought, never won. For the player, the shape is: adventure earns gold under threat; the village converts gold to capability at zero threat, indefinitely, because the campaign never ends. That is the failure mode 3.1 names — not the GM gifting items, but a permanent frictionless market. Recommendation: change — give the village market a supply constraint rather than a risk one. The wares cap and the clock-based restock already exist (below); lean on them rather than treating the village as an unlimited counter. A concrete test: run twenty village turns with 5,000 gp and measure whether the hero can leave arbitrarily equipped.

B. The wares mechanism will not carry "sell your inventory here" — grounded in code. Positions: 3.1 ●, 5.3 ● (uniformity: divergence past a point stops being the same game). fileWare (memory.js:326) files onto _waresWorldNode (memory.js:318), which keys off worldState.world.location only — the settlement node, never the sub-location. Every shop in the village writes to one shared list. That list is capped by settlement size (waresCapFor, WARES_CAP_BY_SIZE = {small:2, medium:4, large:6…}, globals.js:78) and overflow is dropped with node.wares.shift() and a console.warn. Meanwhile fileLocationItem (memory.js:393) does key off currentNodeKey(), which includes the sublocation — so houses are correctly separate. The asymmetry means: items left in a house persist per-house; items sold at the blacksmith land in one village-wide pile of 2–6 that silently evicts the oldest. A player who sells eight items and returns for one will find it gone, with nothing said — which also collides with the project's own no-silent-failures rule. Recommendation: change — either scope wares to the sub-location the way location items already are, or state in the design that shop-sold goods are lossy and surface the eviction to the player. Do not ship the current asymmetry unexamined.

C. The clock keeps its job only if the village costs time. Positions: 1.1 ● ("YOU CAN NOT HAVE A MEANINGFUL CAMPAIGN IF STRICT TIME RECORDS ARE NOT KEPT"), 1.2 ● (time exists to make systems bite), 1.3 ◐ (weak fit: his rationale was multi-party logistics), 7.3 ●. The mute list keeps the clock on and keeps rest, hours and wares on — correct by 1.2, since those are the three things that actually consult it. But with quests, deadlines and schedules muted, nothing in the village spends against a deadline. Per 1.3 and §8, a clock that costs the player fun without costing the character anything is the one he would drop himself. Recommendation: keep as is, with one test — verify that shop hours and the seven-game-day wares restock (WARES_RESTOCK_DAYS, globals.js:77) are the clock's only consumers here, and that a village visit that advances three days visibly changes something. If it does not, the clock in the village is bookkeeping.

D. Residents as playable characters is the strongest part of the proposal. Positions: 3.4 ● (henchmen "usually spell the difference between failure and success", and let play continue without the main PC), 7.2 ◐ (places with inhabitants that persist). Routing "which character do I play" through _switchPlayerCharacter (ui-sheets.js:663) reuses a path that already demotes the old hero to a full NPC with their sheet, migrates relationships and clears activePC. It requires a real charSheet and refuses mid-turn while busy — both honest refusals. The player experience 3.4 predicts is exactly the one described: a retired character keeps existing, mechanically, in a place you can knock on. Recommendation: keep as is. One test worth writing: switch, leave for an adventure, return, and confirm the demoted hero's inventory and relationships survive the library write-back.

E. Muting by kind at one boundary is the right shape; the refusals must state reasons. Positions: 5.3 ● (uniformity is a contract), 5.1 ● ("IT IS THE SPIRIT OF THE GAME, NOT THE LETTER OF THE RULES"), 5.5 ◐ (rule lookup that interrupts play has inverted the priority). One field on the note-registry entry, read once in prompt assembly, is the uniform-table discipline 5.3 asks for. The proposal's mute list (item 5) switches off data.js:108 — "a scene with no stakes is a failed scene" — which this lens names the most Leiberian sentence in the codebase. Turning it off in a no-danger village is right: the rule would manufacture the threat the owner banned. But 1.3 ● is the sharper test and it is unanswered: strip the magic from twenty village turns and what remains is a blacksmith, a trading post and a healer. That is cloak-and-sword, not sword-and-sorcery — the definitional half (5.1 ●) has gone with the danger. What the player would experience is twenty turns of errand prose with no fantasy requirement. Recommendation: the mute list needs a positive replacement clause, not just deletions — one line naming what a village scene is FOR when it is not for danger. Without it the mute list is a subtraction and the GM will fill the vacuum with shopping.

2. The shop roster is a hub, not Lankhmar. Positions: 2.2 ●, 2.3 ◐. The brief's settlements are seven functions — tavern, blacksmith, trading post, alchemist, guild halls, healer, animal handler. 2.2's test is occupations, not services: his Lankhmar is "thick with thieves and shaven priests, lean-framed magicians and fat-bellied merchants". A village populated by shopkeepers is a menu; a village with a boy paid to watch a street is a place. Note also that "guild halls" (plural) imports 2.3's faction generator into a setting with nothing for factions to generate. Recommendation: keep the seven as affordances, but require the village blueprint to seed named residents with trades who sell nothing. Test: read twenty village turns and count how many named figures exist who cannot be transacted with.

3. Muting whispers removes the village's best non-danger engine. Positions: 2.4 ◐/●ƒ, 7.4 ●ƒ. buildWhispersNote (api.js:533) requires a sized node and a source who has been elsewhere — a village of returned adventurers is the ideal case, and the proposal mutes it. The lens's standing question of the whispers system is whether a rumour ever costs anything; the village is the one venue where a rumour arriving wrong is the whole scene and costs nothing but standing. Muting it also silences the only channel by which 7.4's blame — as opposed to fame — could reach a hero between campaigns. Recommendation: do not mute whispers; re-scope them. In kind:"village" the source pool is residents who were in other campaigns, and the fact pool is the returning hero's finished quests. This is the Village Hall (item 6) in dialogue form and needs no new mechanism.

4. Permanence is a ratchet, and the code makes it softer than the brief claims. Positions: 6.1 ◐, 6.2 ●ƒ, 6.3 ●ƒ. "Aye, there's the bitter core of all freedom: no pay!" and "Unlike men, rubies and emeralds do not rest quietly" are the same position: the take does not survive. The village makes the take survive by design, and it is the last place in the product where gold could still be a motive. Concretely: fileLocationItem (memory.js:393) is current-node scoped, stores {name, placed, taken} with no owner field, and taken is a soft flag that never removes the row — placed toggles it back to false. So the brief's (b) "ownership on house nodes" is a genuinely new field, and the brief's (a) gate on [LOCATION_ITEM:…|taken] covers only one of two doors: the player-pickup path runs through autoTakeLocationItem (memory.js:405) off [ITEM_GAINED:], not through the tag the gate refuses. Recommendation: gate both call sites, and add a test that fires the auto-take path in a village-kind campaign. The lens's design ask is narrower than "take things away" (§13, explicitly): it is only that something in the village be worth wanting that gold buys. Storage is not that.

5. The resident-swap is the strongest idea here, and it is the untested claim from §13. Positions: 3.1 ◐, 3.4 ◐, 2.5 ◐/●ƒ, 7.3 ◐. "A new character walks past a retired character's door because the retired character is standing in it" answers 3.4 better than anything in the codebase: companions as people the story is about, not extra actions per turn. And 2.5 — "Modern cities should have ghosts." — reframes the whole feature: a village of your own retired and dead characters is a haunted place without a single danger tag. The clock and location hours stay on in the mute list, so the material for a night self already exists. §13 records that this lens should test, not assert, whether the companion frame reconstructs the pair: the village is the ideal test bed, because with DRIVE muted nothing competes for the turn. Recommendation: add one village-kind ask for an exchange between two residents with the hero as witness (3.3 ◐) and judge the corpus. If it works here, it is evidence for the solo-frame claim generally. Per the brief's own rules this is a prompt-surface change and therefore Fable-tier.

On the three forks

  1. Own campaign vs attached mini-skeleton — own campaign, on 2.1 ◐: his contribution was the city as the adventure; the seam-attached version makes the village literally the road between adventures, which is the arrangement 2.1 says he inverted.
  2. Library as truth vs village as truth — no position on the mechanism (caution 0.2: procedural, send it to Gygax). One observation only, from 9.5 ●: continuity is not bookkeeping, it is what makes anything land — so the fork must end with exactly one writer, and the proposal names one.
  3. Wares session boundary — this lens has no position. 6.3 ●ƒ prefers whichever boundary makes the stock visibly turn over, but that is a preference for change, not for either boundary.

Gaps

  • No Leiber statement on computers, AI or automated narration exists (§14). Nothing here judges the GM-as-model architecture, the mute-list-as-registry design, or write-back.
  • "Fafhrd & the Mouser Say Their Say" (The Dragon #1, 1976) is identified but unread (§14). It is the one document where he wrote his heroes about gaming, and a downtime-home feature is exactly where it would bear. Every gaming position above is therefore ◐ at best.
  • "Fafhrd and Me" is second-hand (§14) — so the co-creation reading behind finding 5 is weaker than it sounds.
  • Whether a safe, questless space is good for a player is a product and wellbeing question. Nothing in the sources touches it, and 7.3 ◐ explicitly forbids this lens from arguing that a place should be sadder or harder to be better fiction.
  • Item-count caps, sync, CAS and migration — out of scope by caution 0.2.

The Moran lens rubric

Caveat: this is a rubric of Jenna Moran's documented positions (DOC/panel/moran_lens.md), not the person. Entry numbers and confidence grades are cited throughout.

1. Verdict

This lens reads the village as a second genre with its own reward list (1.2 ◐) — and the proposal builds the place beautifully while deleting the adventure reward list without writing the village one, so it serves the fiction and not yet the economy.

2. Findings

A. The mute list turns the reward economy off and puts nothing in its seat. Positions: 1.1 ●, 1.2 ◐, 1.4 ◐. MILESTONE_XP={quest:50,boss:100,act:200} (globals.js:67) is the entire payer set, and item 5 mutes quests, combat and act pressure. In a village with no quests and no dangers, the only surviving channel is the GM's flavour [XP:], clamped by GM_XP_CAP_PER_LEVEL=10 (globals.js:69). Entry 1.1 ● holds that XP is "first and foremost a dramatic pacing mechanism"; 1.2 ◐ holds that each genre carries its own list, pastoral paying for relaxing with a friend. What the player experiences: village turns that are pleasant and weightless — nothing tells them a beat ended, so the tempo is entirely the model's. Recommendation: write a village XP list (four or five named scene-kinds), rate-limited the way 1.4 ◐ demands — two awards per visit, clamped like the GM cap — or rule explicitly that the village pays nothing and say so in the player-facing fiction. Per caution 1, the engine is taking the other players' seat as witness; name that substitution in the design doc rather than smuggling it.

B. Muting the montage is correct, and it leaves the fourth button empty. Positions: 5.1 ●, 5.2 ◐, 5.3 ●. montageDue() (game.js) requires MONTAGE_AFTER_TURNS=6 committed turns with no combat, move or rest tag — the village is permanently in that state, so unmuted it would offer "skip ahead" from turn seven onward. Good catch. But engineFourthAction() (game.js:168) is a ladder: rest → answering consumable → accept offer → buy → montage → wildcard. With quests muted, montage and wildcard muted, a healthy hero with no coin standing in a shopless node gets null. Entry 5.3 ● describes exactly this beat — a character out of options fades into the background so the clock can move — but hers pays on the way out. Recommendation: add a village rung (call on a resident, stand a round at the tavern, work at a bench) and pin it with a test that calls engineFourthAction() on a full-HP, zero-gold, zero-quest village state and asserts non-null.

C. Item permanence is already nearly free — and the same code makes silent loss possible. Position: 8.3 ● (the sheet carries the stories lived through; a house is that, spatialized). Grounding: fileLocationItem in memory.js:391–404 never splices — taken is a boolean toggled true, and a later placed flips it back. Node items are append-only for the life of the campaign, so fork 1 buys retention with no new store. The hazard runs the other way: taken has no owner field, so a GM line like "the shelf is bare" flips it with no message. Recommendation: enforce the village-kind refusal inside the tag handler, not at call sites (the registry rule), and make the refusal loud — a toast plus a console warn, never a swallow.

D. Removing obligations is Moran-shaped; removing everything the hero is working on is not. Positions: 2.7 ●, 2.1 ●, 2.2 ●, 2.3 ●. The mute list is a wholesale deletion of obligations — DRIVE, the telegraph, deadlines, stale-quest nudges — and 2.7 ● is explicit that the system should offer payoffs rather than "you should". Strength, and worth recording as such. The risk is 2.3 ●: every character is on at least one quest at all times, and the village makes zero-quest permanent by construction. 2.1 ● reads a quest as "anything that your character is working on", with domestic worked examples (2.2 ●: practise the flute, deal with grief). Recommendation: let the questLog hold village-kind goals the DRIVE rule cannot touch. This is not a ledger by the owner's rule — a ledger is a paper trail as plot, not a quiet stake.

E. In a village of residents, the hero's missing arc becomes the main event. Positions: 4.5 ●, 4.6 ●, 4.3 ●, 4.4 ●. Companions carry wants; the hero carries none. An adventure skeleton hides that; a village with the plot machinery muted cannot, and the player becomes a camera pointed at other people's interiors. Two cheap imports: (i) 4.6 ● — _switchPlayerCharacter (ui-sheets.js:663) already sends a forceful control-reassignment handoff; add one clause naming what the departing hero's last chapter changed in them, which costs no mechanic; (ii) 4.3 ● — [COMPANION_AGENDA_DONE:] (tag_table.js:440) resolves a want for nothing, and in a village whose whole content is residents, an unpaid resolution is now the headline event. Convergence worth recording, not borrowing: 4.4 ● (Issues are GM-assigned, never player-chosen) matches the owner's "never manufacture a want" and "wants are readable, never editable" exactly.

3. On the three forks

  1. Own campaign vs attached mini-skeleton — support own campaign: 4.7 ● treats an ending as a transition ("their next story continues from there"), and the permanence in memory.js:391 comes free only from a campaign that never closes.
  2. Library as truth vs village as truth — no position from this lens's sources; 8.3 ● leans weakly toward whichever preserves the fullest prior-story record, which is write-back, but that is a question, not a verdict.
  3. Wares session boundary — no position. 1.5 ● (frequency encodes weight) only suggests the boundary that makes restock read as an event; departure does that better than a rest, and that is inference, not evidence.

4. Gaps

This lens cannot judge the storage architecture, the sync/CAS path, the picker UX, or the mute-list implementation risk — nothing in the sources touches persistence, tooling or software. It has no statement on AI narration or solo play at all (§11); caution 1's translation is the lens's own construction. Its economy entries are thin: gold, shops, wares and pricing have no entry, so fork 3 is genuinely outside it. And every mechanical claim above graded ◐ comes from a fan digest or reviewer, not the Chuubo's rulebook, which was never read (§11) — 1.2, 1.4 and 5.2 should be checked against the book before any of them drives a build.
