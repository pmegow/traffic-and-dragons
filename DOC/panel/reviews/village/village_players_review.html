The design under review (the brief every persona read)

THE VILLAGE — design under panel review (TODO #6, owner design session 2026-09-12)

The owner's framing (verbatim intent)

  • A single-location campaign.
  • No quests, no dangers.
  • Populated entirely by the player's characters from the character library (every saved character is a resident).
  • Each character has their own quarters — a house, a manor, a hut.
  • A player may leave an item in their quarters and it must stay there until they retrieve it. A robust location-based inventory retention system is required.
  • There is a tavern, a blacksmith, a trading post, an alchemist, guild halls, a healer, an animal handler.
  • Places where a player can empty their inventory and spend gold. Items a player SELLS at a shop last the session but are not granted permanence (unlike items left in a house).
  • The open question the owner raised: how does the player specify WHICH character they experience the village through? "Would a simple 'load character' work? Seems like it SHOULD."

The engine-side answer proposed to the owner (Fable, 2026-09-12)

  1. The village is its own campaign, not an attachment. One persistent campaign in the campaign picker, created from a shipped village blueprint, kind: "village". Characters move between it and adventure campaigns through the character library (which already exists: server-synced, per-account). The August-2026 framing (a "mini-skeleton" that attaches to other campaigns at their seams) is deferred to phase 2 as optional departure/return beats.
  2. Which character you play = the existing hero swap. Every library character enters the village as a resident NPC with a full character sheet (the existing library-import-as-companion path). "Load character" is a picker over the residents that calls the existing _switchPlayerCharacter: it promotes a resident to the hero slot and demotes the previous hero back to a resident. So a new character walks past a retired character's door because the retired character is standing in it.
  3. The library stays the source of truth. The village holds copies. Switching away from a character, or leaving the village for an adventure, writes the sheet back to the library. (Otherwise a sword bought in the village would be missing when a new campaign is started from the library.)
  4. Item retention rides the existing map graph. Every map node already carries an items list and the GM already files [LOCATION_ITEM:name|placed] / taken. Because the village campaign never ends, a house node keeps its items across sessions for free. Additions: (a) a village-kind gate refuses taken unless the hero is the taker (the GM may not invent theft or loss in the village); (b) ownership on house nodes so the HOME block injected on entry lists "your stuff" authoritatively; (c) shop-sold items become that shop's WARES with a turn stamp (the existing wares/hours mechanism) and roll off at a session boundary — proposed boundary: when the hero leaves the village. They never touch node items, which is the permanence distinction.
  5. The mute list — the one real engine change. Every GM turn today gets prompt blocks and engine notes whose job is to push the story forward. With kind: "village" these are switched off at ONE boundary (a field on the note registry entry + the prompt assembly reading the kind once), never by per-site checks: the DRIVE rule and its three-step telegraph; the deadline/commitment observers; agenda births and beats; whispers (rumours); the money-at-stake note; quest-stale, arc-staging, arc-drift and expired-thread nudges; the reckless wildcard and the montage on the fourth button; the combat tracker and death propagation (combat tags refused with an honest reason). What stays on: presence and mood audits, relationship axes, location filing and location items, the clock, wares and hours, the character sheet, rest.
  6. The Village Hall (owner: "A+ idea", 2026-08-24) — finished campaigns are deposited as physical mementos on the hall's walls (a mural, a mounted map, a crown on a plinth), fed by the story-compiler output. Same village store, zero new mechanics. Sequenced after the story compiler (#219 stage 2).

Three forks put to the owner

  1. Village as its own campaign (recommended) vs the attached mini-skeleton.
  2. Library as source of truth with write-back on every switch and departure (recommended) vs the village as truth.
  3. What "session" means for shop wares: until the hero leaves the village (recommended) vs until a long rest.

Product context the panel should hold

  • Solo player, a party of model-narrated companions; the GM is a language model narrating in second person; state lives in tags the model emits and the engine parses (the "anti-drift" stack is the product's validated core).
  • The character library is server-synced and per account; "companion import" already carries cross-campaign relationship history, by owner decree.
  • The game already has: a campaign clock, location hours, a wares economy (shops file wares with prices), rest, relationship axes between characters, presence audits, a fourth engine-authored suggestion button (rest / use consumable / accept offer / buy / montage / reckless wildcard), and a denouement written at a campaign's end.
  • Owner rules that bind: no ledgers as a story device (paper-trail plots are banned in fiction); prose length is not a positive; no silent failures; one uniform registry/table per family of variants (no conditionals sprinkled through call sites); never manufacture a companion "want" — only lived history births one.

Synthesis

Written by Fable after reading all five persona reviews, with the nine-lens synthesis already in hand. The players agree with the designers on the structure and add what the designers could not see: the first twenty seconds, the phone, the car, and the count.

The verdict in one line

Five players who play in five different ways all want the same two things from the village that the proposal does not yet give them: to be recognised on arrival, and a village that ran while they were away. Everything else they say sharpens the designers' findings rather than contradicting them.

What the players add that the lenses did not
  1. The return is the product. Rosa's Car Mode recap speaks a chapter summary that a plotless campaign never produces; Nan's phone with the sound off has no recap at all; Gerry wants a resident who remembers a fact from a campaign two years gone. A kind:"village" return greeting, triggered on real elapsed time, rendered as prose in the first turn, naming one specific thing from this player's record. Test: close the tab for four days, reopen, and require the greeting to name the thing.
  2. Nobody says no. Ines names the agreeable GM as the thing that ends solo campaigns, and the village removes danger without adding refusal. Test: a fifteen-turn village run, count refusals, grudges and "not today"; zero is a failed design for this player. This is the players' version of the lenses' "cut on danger, not initiative."
  3. The stash must be seen, not just stored. Bert wants the grouped inventory renderer pointed at the house node so he can sort and count; Nan wants a placed object to change the house's description so the prose names it unprompted; Gerry wants the HOME block to make permanence visible. Data for the panel, prose for the room, and both from one write.
  4. The count is the contract. Bert leaves a marked item to test the promise; a wrong count once and he is gone. The same six code findings the lenses raised, plus one more from Gerry: a node merge in the identity layer re-keys a house and its chest moves or vanishes. Add the merge case to the pre-build test list.
  5. The fourth button as a shop bell. Three of five players walk the ladder and land on Buy or nothing. Nan names a permanent Buy button as the exact thing that makes a cozy place feel engineered. A village rung above buy is not polish; it is the difference between a home and a storefront.
  6. The Hall is empty for most players. Gerry: "finished" fires only on the fourth death or the accepted ending, and his campaigns stopped rather than ended. Ines: half of solo campaigns never conclude. Nan: bare walls on day one. Three fixes, all cheap: a File-menu close for a stopped campaign; a wall for the unfinished, named not resolved; and the Hall seeded on day one from the library itself, one object per retired resident. Gerry's stronger form: the Hall's primary content is a fate line per resident, and the engine already builds that block for the denouement.
  7. The epilogue wants a player's line. Gerry: one optional player-authored sentence per retired resident about what they do now, stored on the library character as canon. It satisfies the never-manufacture-a-want rule more cleanly than generation.
  8. Voice is where the village is hardest. Rosa: four narrated options read as a menu of errands; a misheard "take the sword" moves an item out of a house she cannot see, and there is no spoken undo; eight residents in one scene is the voice bank's real test. A spoken "never mind" scoped to the last item move, and a listening test with a full room, before the village ships in Car Mode.
  9. Prices must hold still. Bert: a 3× price band reads as regional variation across three towns and as invention at one blacksmith visited weekly. Pin village prices to the bible value or anchor the GM on the last quote.
  10. The DRIVE directive lives in two places. Ines found a second push instruction in the stable prompt preamble, outside the note registry. The kind gate must select a preamble variant from a table as well, and the stable-half byte-identity check applies.
Where the players and the lenses agree

Own campaign, unanimous across fourteen reviewers. Substitute rather than mute, and keep the residents' initiative. Residency as a non-party state. The HOME block as data, not a manifest. Wares on the campaign clock: two players who never leave the village, Rosa and Nan, pointed out that "until the hero leaves" means the shops never restock for them, which settles fork 3 from the other side.

The evidence hole the panel declares

There is no independent account anywhere in the reachable corpus of low-stakes, slice-of-life play with an AI GM, only vendor marketing; every cozy source is a visual game; and no first-person account of playing a text RPG while driving was found. The village's core thesis, that a place made of your own retired characters is not arbitrary, is a position the panel can state and cannot support from data. It has to be tested in the wild, on the owner's own campaigns, before the second act is built on it.

What changes in the design

To the nine-lens list add: a return greeting keyed on elapsed time; a refusal count as an acceptance test; the stash rendered in the panel and reflected in the house description; the identity-merge case in the permanence tests; the Hall seeded from the library on day one with a wall for the unfinished and a fate line per resident; a player-authored line per retired resident; a spoken undo and a full-room listening test for Car Mode; pinned village prices; and the preamble variant beside the note gate.

Rosa Pike — the Commuter persona

1. Day one

She parks, opens the app, and says "what was I doing?" to the car before she asks the game anything — that is the tell (persona Identity). The village answers well: she can say "go to the tavern" at a red light and nothing in the scene punishes a thirty-second gap, which is exactly the shape her 25-minute, hands-off, ends-mid-scene session needs (R5 ○, R3 ●). Where day one fails her is the first thirty seconds: she resumes into a campaign that, by design, has no plot — and the resume machinery she depends on is built to recap plot.

2. Findings

The recap speaks story; the village only has state. Positions: R1 (◐ Spotify recap framing + ◐ vendor retention data, with Lane C #6 ● "not knowing what to do next" as the killer); R2 ●. The proposal mutes arc-staging, quest-stale and beat machinery for kind:"village". Car Mode's resume bookend calls carRecapText() (helpers.js:2139), which is literally "Previously: " + last chapter summary + " You are at X." — and falls back to "No chapters yet — this is the beginning." A campaign with no arcs may close no chapters for weeks, so the driver who most needs re-entry gets either a stale line or the beginner's line, forever. Recommendation: a village-kind recap that speaks state — who greeted you last, what is in your quarters, gold, which resident's door you were at — and a test that resumes a 40-turn village after PREVIOUSLY_AFTER_MS and asserts the spoken text is not the empty-chapter fallback.

The fourth button is her only usable inventory verb, and the mute list is near it. Positions: R10 ○ (inferred), R2 ●, R6 ●. engineFourthAction() (game.js:168) offers buy when a ware is live in the scene — one sentence she can accept with one word, the only inventory interaction that survives having no eyes. The proposal mutes the montage and wildcard rungs but keeps wares and hours, so buy should survive; nothing in the brief says so explicitly. Her bag grows because lists are unreadable by ear, not because she is a hoarder — a friction fix aimed at Bert misses her entirely. Recommendation: state the buy rung as retained in the mute-list spec, and prototype one spoken bulk action ("sell everything I'm not wearing") that reads back a total, never an itemised list. Grade this as a hypothesis: R10 is ○.

Four numbered options is the storybook shape audio players named as the problem. Positions: R6 ● (RPG most-wanted and most-underserved; parser beats choice-GUI), R2 ●. buildOptionsSpeech() (helpers.js:1080) emits "Option 1: … Option 2: …". In a dungeon those four options are the game. In a village of shops and residents, four engine-suggested options per turn will read as a menu of errands, and R6's evidence is that audio-first players reject narrated menus while wanting depth. Recommendation: keep the buttons, but test a village turn with options not auto-read — mic straight after narration — and compare. Free spoken input is the accessible shape.

Permanence plus misheard speech is a new failure mode. Positions: R4 ● (misheard basics, unintended action selected), R3 ●. The command vocabulary in ui-carmode.js is pick / repeat / repeat-all / wrap-up / recap / roll. There is no spoken undo. The proposal's gate (item 4a) refuses taken unless the hero is the taker — it does not help when STT puts the words in the hero's mouth. A misheard "take the sword" permanently moves an item out of a house she cannot see. Recommendation: a spoken "undo that" / "never mind" scoped to the last LOCATION_ITEM move in village campaigns. R4 argues the village is the right place to harden voice input, not a reason to skip it.

All-conversation content is where the voice bank either proves itself or exposes itself. Positions: R7 ● (full cast vs one narrator, genuinely split; one weak voice "ruins everything"), panel fork 4.2 ●. The village is residents talking — six or eight speakers in a scene, auto-cast by [SAY:] and the #402 pins. She loses track if voices are identical and may find it theatrical if they all differ; there is no safe default. Recommendation: keep as is, and run one village scene with eight residents as a listening test before shipping. Also confirm speech rate persists across Car Mode sessions (R8 ●) — she listens at 1.4×.

3. Comes back / walks away

Comes back: hearing that the old soldier from the mountain campaign has the house by the mill — told, not shown (persona: retired characters). Walks away: a tag, bracket or UI string read aloud in the car, which collapses the illusion faster than bad prose ever could (R2 ●, Lane OWN O14 ●).

4. On the three forks

  1. Own campaign vs attached mini-skeleton: weakly for its own campaign — R3 ● says the boundary is every session, and a mode that flips mid-drive is a boundary she cannot see coming. Low confidence; no sourced position on campaign topology.
  2. Library as source of truth: this persona has no position.
  3. Wares session boundary: she never leaves the village, so "until the hero leaves" means wares never roll for her — prefer a boundary she can be told about in one sentence. Rests on R10 ○; a question, not a verdict.

5. Gaps

R5 ○ — "a no-danger setting is the one that fits driving" is the strongest product argument this persona has and the least sourced; no first-person account of playing a text RPG while driving was found, so it cannot certify the village's premise. R10 ○ — no source describes voice-driven inventory, so the sell-in-one-sentence recommendation is a test to run, not a requirement. forum.audiogames.net was unreachable behind Cloudflare, and Reddit's commuting and driving communities are hard-blocked, so the evidence skews to AppleVis (iOS) and interactive fiction.

Ines Halloran — the Oracle-Keeper persona

A composite persona (panel_of_players.md §5) — not a person, not a user. Positions cited by number and grade; ○ positions inform questions only.

1. Day one

She opens the village at a desk on a Tuesday evening, notebook beside the laptop, expecting 45–90 minutes. The first thing she does is not play — she reads the resident list, because her library holds the characters she has carried, and she wants to see who is here and who is where (I6 ●). Then she loads one and walks out into the lane. The proposal serves that first twenty minutes very well: the doors are her own people, and the character-library-as-cast is the strongest version of "a hub matters because of the people in it" (panel 3.4 ●). Where it fails her is at minute thirty, when she has met everyone and there is no next thing and no one has refused her anything.

2. Findings

The DRIVE rule is not where the mute list thinks it is. Positions: I1 ●, I4 ●, I9 ●. Proposal §5 switches the push-forward machinery off "at ONE boundary (a field on the note registry entry + the prompt assembly reading the kind once)". But the most forceful drive directive is not a note. It is hardcoded in the stable system-prompt preamble at api.js:2280 — the GM is told to write prose that keeps the player in danger and to "push hooks and threats, never wait to be entertained". Muting the observers leaves that sentence running every turn. What she experiences: a model straining against its own instructions, inventing a travelling circus to fill a quiet evening — her named failure, randomness fatigue (I1 ●). Recommendation: the campaign kind must select a preamble variant from a table alongside the note gate, and the change needs the stable-half byte-identity check the drift policy already demands.

Muting agendas removes the logic engine, not the danger. Positions: I1 ●, I9 ●, I10 ●. §5 mutes "agenda births and beats" while keeping presence, mood and relationship axes. But an agenda is a resident wanting something, and a defined setting acting as a logic engine — factions with goals, named people with motivations — is precisely what she believes coherence comes from (I1 ●). Presence tells her the baker is in the bakery; it does not tell her the baker wants anything. What she experiences: a village of shopkeepers waiting to be spoken to, which her file names as the exact failure case. Recommendation: do not cut agendas. Cut their stakes — keep births and wants, mute the deadline observers, the commitment clock and the telegraph. The fork to put to the owner is "agendas with no stakes", not "no agendas".

Nothing in the mute list gives a resident the power to say no. Positions: I4 ●, I3 ●, panel 3.5 ●. Danger is removed by design; refusal is not the same thing and is not added anywhere. Her first and largest objection to AI GMs is the relentlessly agreeable one that resolves everything into permanent peace. What she experiences concretely: she asks the smith for a favour on turn 6, turn 40 and turn 200, and gets it three times. The village becomes a vending machine with dialogue. Recommendation: a test, not a feature argument — script a 15-turn village run and count refusals, grudges and "not today". If the count is zero, the design has already failed for this player, and no amount of item permanence repairs it.

Item permanence rests on a call that fails silently today. Positions: I5 ●, panel 3.2 ●. §4 says retention rides the existing map graph "for free". In memory.js:393-397, fileLocationItem returns with no warning when the node is unknown, and taken only flips a flag rather than removing the entry. A player leaving a sword in a house the graph has not yet minted loses the filing with no message — against the project's own no-silent-failures rule. She would not notice the sword; she would notice that the record was wrong, and a wrong record is how the fourth-session collapse starts for her (I5 ●). Recommendation: the village-kind gate in §4(a) must refuse loudly, with a reason surfaced to the player, and an engine test must place an item at an unvisited house node.

The Hall honours the wrong half of the campaigns. Positions: I7 ●, I2 ●. §6 deposits finished campaigns as mementos, fed by the story compiler. Roughly half of surveyed campaigns never reach a satisfying ending (I7 ●), and her own longest was paused with intent to return, not concluded. Separately, removing quests removes the answer to "what do I do next" — the named killer of solo campaigns (I2 ●) — while §5 mutes two of the six suggestion-button branches. Recommendation: let a paused campaign deposit a memento too, marked as unfinished; and re-check the button set that survives the mute, since those buttons are now load-bearing in a way they never were.

3. Comes back / walks away

Comes back: a resident who wanted something last week and has moved on it without her — the village ran while she was away. Walks away: the fourth evening where nobody refuses her anything and the GM invents an event to fill the silence.

4. On the three forks

Village as its own campaign vs attached mini-skeleton: own campaign — a place with its own logic running while she is not looking cannot be a seam in someone else's story (I1 ●, I7 ●). Library as source of truth: this persona has no sourced position on where truth lives; her only stake is that the record be consistent, and write-back on every switch serves that (I5 ●). What "session" means for wares: this persona has no position — items barely register for her and she sells nothing.

5. Gaps

Three things this persona cannot carry. Her central want — a persistent home base you return to — is assembled, not read: no thread was found in which a solo player describes one, so treat the "place" half as ○ and the Day-one section above as a question rather than a finding. There is no independent account of low-stakes AI-GM play anywhere in the corpus (vendor marketing only), so I10's "cozy is a real subgenre" can raise the arbitrariness critique but cannot settle fork 4.5. And no AI-GM retrospective longer than a few sessions was reachable, which means I5's claim that a forever-campaign is the anti-drift stack's hardest test is a shape claim I would stand behind and a magnitude claim I would not. Finally, r/Solo_Roleplay is hard-blocked; the solo evidence here skews to people who write publicly about their play, which is not the median solo player.

Bert Kovac — the Quartermaster persona

Composite persona, not a person. Every position below is cited by entry number and confidence grade; ○ positions inform questions only.

1. Day one

Desktop, Saturday afternoon, three hours, the item bible open in a second window. He does not walk the village — he opens the trading post, the blacksmith and the alchemist in the first ten minutes and reads all three stock lists before speaking to anyone, then dumps his adventure inventory into his own house because the brief promised it stays there. The proposal serves that opening better than any feature the game has shipped: a house node that never expires is the first place his stuff has ever been safe (B5 ●). Where it fails him is the second hour, when he tries to find one thing in what he put there, and when the third shop's stock list turns out to be the same list as the first two.

2. Findings

One stock list, capped at two, for seven shops. Positions: B3 ● (no sink / vendor trash is one complaint), B9 ● (he will not spend what he cannot price), panel 3.3 ● (witnessing is not playing). fileWare (memory.js:326) files to the world node — _waresWorldNode resolves worldState.world.location, and the shop only survives as a row.at string. Every ware in the village shares one array, capped by waresCapFor → WARES_CAP_BY_SIZE (globals.js:78), which is unknown:2 for an unsized node and small:2. The proposal then routes player-sold items into that same array. Concretely: he sells four things at the trading post, and the blacksmith's stock is silently evicted — eviction is a console.warn (memory.js:336), invisible to him. He opens the blacksmith on visit two, sees his own sold junk, and stops opening shop lists — the exact vendor-hopping death B3 documents. Recommendation: wares belong on the sublocation node in kind:"village", with a per-shop cap. Test first: file one ware from each of seven shops and assert seven survive.

Two potions in a house become one potion. Positions: B2 ● (the stash is a room he tidies), B5 ● (persistence failure reads as betrayal), owner rule no silent failures. fileLocationItem (memory.js:393) matches on lowercased name: placing a second "Healing potion" finds the existing row and sets taken=false rather than adding a row. There is no count. Worse, the function opens if(!memory.map.nodes[key])return; — a placement into a house the GM has not formally filed is dropped with no warning, where the sibling fileLocationState creates the node. He is the player who deliberately tests this: leave a marked item, come back, check. He will find the count wrong, and a wrong count is the one failure he was watching for. Recommendation: quantity or instance keys, node creation on placement, and a sabotage test that proves the drop is loud.

A price band of 3× is not a price. Positions: B9 ●, plus the persona's second trust-breaker (prices that move without a reason). item_bible.js:36-37 states that a [WARES:] price outside 3× of value is warned about, never rewritten. In an adventure campaign, three towns, that reads as regional variation. In a village he visits every week with the same blacksmith, a 25 gp axe legitimately quoting 12 then 40 is the model making things up. Recommendation: in kind:"village", pin shop prices to the bible value, or surface the last-quoted price in the HOME block so the GM is anchored and he can see the anchor.

The stash has no surface, only prose. Positions: B2 ●, B7 (display half ●, item half ○), B10 ●, panel 4.3 ●. updateInvPanel (ui-panels.js:258) renders activePlayer().inventory through the bible-driven collapsible groups. Node items reach only the GM, via the geo block (api.js:43); nothing player-facing renders them. So four hundred items in a house is a paragraph the GM writes from a list he cannot sort, filter or count. His own file names this as the largest open question about him — whether B2's pleasure survives when the stash is a sentence. Recommendation: reuse the existing grouped inventory renderer for the current house node's items. It is the cheapest thing on this list and it converts storage into the activity he actually enjoys.

The fourth button goes quiet in the village, and "buy" is the only rung left. Positions: B1 ● (scarcity produces paralysis), B3 ●. engineFourthAction (game.js:168): rest needs hp<maxHp/2, use-consumable needs wounded or a condition, accept needs an offered quest, montage and wildcard are on the mute list. In a no-danger village, all of those are dead by construction — the button is buy or null, gated by the two-ware cap above. That is coherent, but it means the engine's one nudge toward spending is throttled by an invisible cap. Recommendation: keep the mute list as written; fix the cap first and the button becomes the village's economy prompt for free.

3. Comes back / walks away

Comes back: the sword he has carried since turn forty came out of the house exactly as he left it, and the blacksmith can make it better rather than sell him a replacement (B6 ●). Walks away: an item count that is wrong once — not lost, just wrong — because that is the only thing he was checking (B5 ●, panel 3.1 ●).

4. On the three forks

  1. Village as its own campaign vs attached mini-skeleton: own campaign. B5 ● — permanence has to be a place he can point at, and a campaign that never ends is the only promise the engine can keep without a new mechanism.
  2. Library as source of truth: yes, with write-back. B5 ● again — his nightmare is the retired character's equipment list coming up empty, and the library is the record that outlives the village.
  3. What "session" means for shop wares: this persona has no sourced position on the boundary, but B8 ● objects to the shape — clock expiry (WARES_RESTOCK_DAYS, 7 days) plus a leave-the-village roll-off is two rules on one array. Pick one.

5. Gaps

B7's item half is ○ — no thread was read in which a player displays a sentimental item, so "let him hang the sword on the wall" is a question to the owner, not a verdict, and the Village Hall memento design cannot be scored from this file. B10 ● says friction is the only thing that makes him let go, but whether removing it is a gift or the end of his economy is panel fork 4.3, graded ● / ○ and unresolved — this review deliberately does not answer it. And the persona's own largest gap applies squarely here: every stash source is a CRPG with an inventory grid, and there is no evidence about stashes in a text game. Finding 4 is the persona's strongest instinct and its least transferable one.

Nan Oyelaran — the Twenty-Minute Regular persona

Composite persona, Panel of Players. Positions cited by entry number and grade. Not a person, not a user, not a prevalence claim.

1. Day one

She opens the village at 11:40pm, phone, one thumb, sound off, and types something like "go to the tavern." What the proposal gives her that first session is real and rare: the person behind the bar is a character she made and retired, and nothing in the room is going to try to kill her (N1 ●, N7 ●). What it does not give her is the twenty seconds that decide whether she comes back — nobody says her name, nothing tells her what she did here last time, and by the second or third exchange the engine's fourth button is offering her something to buy.

2. Findings

① The fourth button collapses into a shop bell. Positions: the trust list's "being sold to" (Lane OWN O2 ●); N2 ●; N3 ●. The mute list (proposal §5) switches off the montage and the reckless wildcard. Walk the actual ladder in game.js engineFourthAction() (~L168–186) with those two gone and no danger in the world: rest needs hp below half — never; use consumable needs wounded or a condition — never; accept the offer needs a quest — banned by design; which leaves {kind:"buy", text:"Buy the …"} whenever wares are live in the scene, and null otherwise. So the engine-authored button she sees every turn in her home village is either a purchase prompt or blank. For a player whose named curdle-condition is a place that feels engineered for engagement, a permanent Buy button is the worst possible residue of a mute list. Recommendation: add a village rung above buy before shipping — a visit, a greeting, a "put the box on the shelf." Do not let the ladder fall through to commerce by subtraction.

② No part of the engine knows she was here yesterday — in text. Positions: N1 ●, N6 ●; panel 3.1 ●. Test it by closing the tab for four days and reopening.

③ Muting is subtraction, and the freed slots refill with bookkeeping. Positions: N3 ● ("can quickly become boring"); N4 ●. 1. Day one

He opens the village on his Tuesday evening, and the first thing he does is look for the Village Hall — because that is the room that decides whether this place is his binder or someone's screensaver. 2. Findings

The hall is fed by an event that almost never fires. Positions: G1 ●, G5 ●, panel 3.4 ●. Mementos come from finished campaigns via the story compiler. In the engine, "finished" means exactly two things: the fourth death (deathSceneOnwardConfirm) or the player accepting the spine-complete offer (endingDecide) — both in game.js around lines 3318–3355. There is no player-invoked close. His three abandoned campaigns produce nothing, forever. Recommendation: ship a File-menu "close this campaign" that sets ended/denouementOwed from the record, plus a hall wall for the unfinished — a shelf of open books, named, not resolved. Without it the hall's population is zero for most players indefinitely.

A fate list, not a mural. Positions: G2 ●, panel 3.4 ●. The brief's mementos are objects: a mural, a mounted map, a crown on a plinth. The sourced request is smaller and different — a status line per known character between chapters, and GMs listing what became of each survivor years later. The engine already assembles exactly this: buildDenouementCompanions() (api.js:738) emits each living companion's bond to the hero, their motivation, and their still-unfinished want. Recommendation: make the per-resident fate line the hall's primary content and the objects its decoration. Reuse that block; do not author a second one.

The epilogue is written by the wrong author. Positions: G3 ●, G4 ●. DENOUEMENT_SYS (api.js:706) has the model write the closing 300–500 words, tightly fenced against invention (#394's trait/flaw guard is good work). But the sourced value of an epilogue is that it is the player's one undiced say. In the village the natural form is one line the player writes about what their retired character does now. Recommendation: one optional player-authored line per resident, stored on the library character and injected as canon. It also satisfies the owner's never-manufacture-a-want rule more cleanly than generation does.

The permanence gate has no field to test, and the stash lives in memory. Positions: G7 ●, panel 3.2 ●. The brief refuses taken "unless the hero is the taker" — but [LOCATION_ITEM:name|taken] carries no actor (tag_table.js:544; the prompt doc at line 130 says player pickup is auto-handled by [ITEM_GAINED:]). In village kind the only honest rule is: refuse taken outright. Worse, node items live in memory.map.nodes[key].items behind locResolve (memory.js:393–403) — an identity-layer merge re-keys the node and his chest moves or evaporates with no error. That is the trust break the panel rates ● twice. Recommendation: failing test first — merge two village nodes, assert the house's items survive and are attributed; and make the HOME block on entry list "your stuff" so permanence is visible, not merely implemented.

The mute list removes the machinery that made answers specific. Positions: G6 ●, G8 ●, panel 3.3 ●. Muting DRIVE, agendas, whispers, montage and combat is the right smaller claim (G8 ● argues for shipping the narrow thing first). But what remains — presence, mood, relationships — is precisely where a model is generically right. And the memory tiers will keep summarizing: the era prompt asks for "major plot movements... decisive outcomes" (memory.js:1610), which a village does not produce, so chapters degrade into pleasant nothing. Recommendation: a village-kind chapter prompt that records who was seen, what was said and what was decided; and one specificity instrument — a test asserting a resident's greeting names a fact only this player's record contains.

3. Comes back / walks away

Comes back: a resident who remembers something specific from a campaign that ended two years ago, and is right about it. Walks away: something he left in a house is gone, or changed, and nothing in the app explains why.

  2. Library as source of truth — yes, with a visible receipt on every write-back. G5 ●: continuity loss is a first-order cause of campaign death, and a silent sync failure here is exactly that.
