What should the map know?

The atlas should show the world the player has been introduced to. Today, a generated map can introduce its own world first.

Make player knowledge authoritative.

I agree with your concern. A named hut is a fact about the world, an invitation to act, and—in this case—a suggestion that a person named Stalgind exists. It should appear on the player’s map only after the GM has established what the party knows about it.

I would change the authority and discovery model while retaining the mapping technology. The generator is useful for drawing, spatial consistency, and optional private preparation. Its output currently has more authority than the narrative contract can support.

The narrower rule I recommend is: every meaningful feature on the player map needs a recorded source of player knowledge. That source can be a description, a visible landmark, a reliable report, a purchased map, or an explicit starting briefing. A unique building or named landmark needs that evidence. Anonymous forest texture can follow an established statement that this is a forest.

A GM can prepare a hut before the party discovers it. Preparation, existence, discovery, and knowing its name are separate facts. The current system largely combines them at the point where the fog lifts.

The engineering worth preserving

The nesting registry, bounded document caches, explicit name matching, scoped room identities, save recipes, fingerprint checks, error reporting, and separation of pure reads from campaign writers are useful foundations. The view is a relatively thin shell. The GM-only digest is deliberately excluded from Table Talk’s shared geography block, which is a sound audience boundary.

The current approach also avoids asking a language model to regenerate arbitrary coordinates every turn. That is valuable: a new prose flourish should not move an inn, change the exit layout, or redraw yesterday’s road.

The problem is larger than one label.

HIGH · DISCLOSURE

Geographic proximity is being treated as knowledge.

A circle around travelled ground can be a useful exploration abstraction. It cannot establish that a hut was visible through trees at night, that its entrance was found, or that the party learned its owner’s name. The screenshot is explicitly a night scene. Fog currently considers none of those distinctions.

The same policy reaches labels, clicks, cards and, in the current checkout, keyboard selection. Hiding a label alone would leave other ways to discover the object. A future disclosure policy must be applied before all player-facing projections.

HIGH · AUTHORITY

The map can overrule an authored scene.

“where it and a description above disagree about layout, distance or direction, the map is right”

This is the current MAP instruction. Meanwhile LOCATION_DESC is taught as permanent, write-once canon. The project already tracks the arrival-order conflict in TODO #694: a settlement is described before its detailed sheet exists, then the next prompt gives the new map priority.

The start classifier improves the broad fit—forest instead of village, coast instead of inland—but it does not encode the clearing’s authored roads, marsh edge, Pact stones, and southern arch as constraints. Choosing a forest sheet is insufficient to map this particular clearing. The wording “the story’s own description placed it there” overstates how much of that description was actually mapped.

HIGH · NARRATIVE COVERAGE

Introduced landmarks have no general route onto the drawing.

In an isolated copy of the saved campaign, fileExit("The Waystone Arch", …) succeeded. Following the unchanged location pointer left the atlas unchanged and atlasFind(..., "The Waystone Arch") still returned null. The narrative and drawing are doing exactly what their separate APIs promise.

Further, atlasAdopt() has addition branches for region, town, interior, and dungeon, but no wild-feature branch. An absent sub-location under a wild sheet can be refused as “a place cannot be added to a wild,” even though the library supports authored wild-feature edits. Thus the region most relevant here is especially poorly served by story-driven additions.

The reverse mismatch is visible in the numbers: the generated Bear Spring is about 338 m from the placeholder centre and the Boar Tree about 149 m. They are outside the 100 m reveal despite being discussed in the screenshot; the unmentioned hut is inside it. “Explored ground” and “known landmark” need separate representations.

HIGH · SEMANTIC PLACEMENT

Some fallbacks invent meaning, not just coordinates.

The start’s type is classified, but a newly named world place later in play still defaults to a village when it lacks a kind hint. The saved campaign contains an actual example: The Ironwood Heart was added at turn 6 as a village. Its blueprint describes living halls and a court reached westward from the clearing; the rebuilt wild-sheet digest places the Heart 20 km north of the sheet. This is evidence of competing spatial authorship, even if “settlement” might be a defensible classification of that court.

An unsupported kind or unresolved placement should remain explicit uncertainty. It should not quietly become a village or gain a precise nearby location simply because the map needs something drawable.

MEDIUM · SPATIAL CERTAINTY

An approximate position produces exact-looking disclosure.

The “somewhere here” label is honest about uncertainty, but its coordinates are still consumed as a real observation point. That is the immediate amplifier in the hut case. The visit record wisely avoids persisting a centre as an exact stand; the live reveal nevertheless uses it.

Travel fog also derives routes from pairs of visited endpoints using the map’s network. That is an intentional approximation, not a record of every step narrated. A cautious route, detour, teleport, or underground journey needs an explicit travel interpretation before the drawing is allowed to disclose a corridor of ground.

atlas.js:325–395, 1290–1296, 1466–1500; tndmaps.js:6374–6535. This is a design limitation; the existing underground-travel safeguards are real and should be retained.

Secondary visual observation: the long party label clips at the right edge in both the attachment and the reproduction. That is a separate presentation issue; fixing it would not resolve the authority or discovery problems.

Which kind of game is the map serving?

A generated world can be a valid foundation for a game. In a geographic exploration campaign, the map can be prepared first and the GM can referee that fixed world. Stable places, meaningful distance, routes, and discoveries then give the player something to reason about. The hut has a legitimate role if it belongs to that prepared world and the GM introduces it when appropriate.

That approach has obligations: the authored adventure must fit the prepared geography; the GM must know the destination before describing arrival; a public map must disclose only what the party has learned; and generated names must not silently reveal identities. If those obligations were met, I would not object to preparing huts ahead of time.

Traffic and Dragons currently promises a stronger role for authored premises and narrated play. Its blueprints name meaningful places and directions. Its descriptions are durable canon. The player’s primary interface is the GM’s prose or voice. A visual feature can compete with that interface by implying “this matters; go here” before the GM has made it part of the scene. It also gives a player looking at the map information unavailable to a player listening in Car Mode.

For that product, I recommend a map that documents established geography and assists the GM’s preparation. It should reward attention to the narrative, provide orientation, and preserve spatial memory. The renderer should have freedom to draw a forest attractively; the content generator should have a clear boundary around proposing a particular hut.

The appropriate boundary is consequence. A generic tree stamp conveys terrain already established. A singled-out tree, bridge, spring, hut, camp, ruin, or doorway invites a decision and therefore needs a source. Even a blank house silhouette can imply a usable shelter; anonymous buildings should only appear where the scene has established an ordinary built environment.

A proposed contract: world, knowledge, drawing.

Represent these as distinct concerns over shared identities. This does not require replacing the project’s identity system or building another independent location graph.

  1. World factsAuthored canon and accepted GM decisions. Private prepared contents may exist here. Generated suggestions remain proposals until accepted.
  2. Player knowledgeWhat was disclosed, when, by whom or by what observation; which name and attributes are known; and how certain they are.
  3. Map projectionStable geometry, terrain texture, symbols and routes. Draw only the facts this audience can know, at the precision the evidence supports.

One feature identity; several things we may know about it

A feature can carry a canonical location key or stable feature ID, its container, kind, accepted facts, source references, placement constraints, and disclosure records. The player-facing name and certainty are derived from those records. Keep the schema uniform across feature types, with one adapter registry for each drawable scale.

What the GM establishesWhat the map may show
“A low roof is visible through the trees to the west.”A building or roof marker to the west, at approximate range. No owner’s name or interior.
“That is Stalgind’s hut,” says a local guide.The name as reported knowledge, with its source retained. A report need not be infallible.
The party arrives and identifies the dwelling.A visited hut, with supported identity and position. Existing notes stay attached to the same feature.
A blueprint privately prepares the hut.Nothing on the player map until a public disclosure. The GM may use its prepared facts.
The generator proposes an incidental hut.A private suggestion the GM may accept or ignore. It has no automatic public or canonical status.

Commit narration and discoveries together

The GM should return structured introductions and disclosures alongside the prose through the existing turn-commit architecture. The engine validates identities, containers, source references and permitted transitions, then updates the narrative geography and player map from that accepted event. A turn that says “you see a hut” should be able to put a hut on the map immediately, without moving the party into it.

A hidden tag alone is insufficient evidence that the player was told. Link a public disclosure to a visible narration span or an explicit player-facing discovery receipt. A structural validator can check that the referenced span exists; it cannot perfectly prove semantic agreement. Keep unsupported disclosures out of the map, surface the mismatch for correction, and test the narration-plus-disclosure contract with representative model outputs. Avoid relying on a second model call every turn to infer what the first one meant.

Use existing descriptions and exits as evidence, not as text to rescan indefinitely for nouns. A later correction updates the accepted fact and its projections explicitly; it does not rewrite the original transcript. The immutable first description remains a historical record rather than an automatic veto on every legitimate later discovery.

Let geometry express uncertainty

A relation such as “spring southwest of the clearing” is useful before an exact distance exists. Draw an approximate marker or a schematic connection. Reserve exact coordinates and scale claims for accepted geometry. An unresolved party location can be represented by its named area or a visibly uncertain region; it should not reveal a circle around an arbitrary centre.

The mapping library can propose coordinates that satisfy known bearings, adjacency, floors, doors, and terrain. Once accepted, those coordinates remain stable. If new constraints cannot fit, report the conflict and keep the prior facts. The renderer should never silently move established places or overrule an authored westward road to obtain a nicer drawing.

Discovery is also broader than travel. Distant landmarks, a purchased map, or reliable directions can reveal information outside walked ground. Conversely, walking within 100 metres of a private feature does not automatically identify it. Keep travelled area, observed terrain, known landmarks, and reported routes distinct.

Keep GM preparation private and bounded

The GM needs committed world facts, recent disclosures, and relevant nearby possibilities. A digest of an entire procedurally populated sheet currently gives every detail equal authority. Separate established canon from optional suggestions and retrieve only useful nearby material. If a campaign deliberately adopts a prepared map, its committed hidden contents remain authoritative for the GM while the player projection stays filtered.

For arrivals that need a prepared interior or town plan, resolve the intended destination before the arrival prose is written. A topological description can otherwise precede precise rendering, provided the renderer obeys that description. Either approach removes the current sequence where a freshly generated destination contradicts the description just saved.

What this scene should communicate

After the supplied exploration reply, the player knows about the Pact stones, the great split tree to the north, a spring southwest, and the arch on the southern crest. Those are useful map content whether or not all have been visited. The hut has no demonstrated disclosure in the available evidence.

If the GM later introduces a hut, the same feature can be added or revealed at that point. If the party already learned about it in an unavailable turn, that disclosure would justify showing the supported facts. Absence from the original area description alone is not a permanent ban on new discoveries.

A practical transition, if we adopt this direction

  1. Choose the authority rule first. For ordinary blueprint and narrative campaigns, authored facts and accepted play constrain the map; player disclosure controls its public contents. Offer a map-governed campaign only as an explicit product choice if that experience is wanted.
  2. Introduce the knowledge projection. Establish one read boundary for symbols, labels, tooltips, cards, keyboard choices, exports and player-help context. Include geometry in the filter: a hidden hut’s footprint or private doorway must not survive behind a removed label. Keep the GM’s private view separate.
  3. Connect introduced places, independently of movement. Extend the existing event/identity boundary so a described landmark, exit or building can be recorded without arrival. Carry kind and placement evidence consistently; support wild features through the library’s existing edit surface.
  4. Constrain generation and change prompt authority together. Separate decorative terrain from semantic features. Admit optional generated features through the GM’s preparation/disclosure flow. Remove the unconditional “map is right” conflict rule only with a replacement that explains established facts, proposals and uncertainty.
  5. Migrate conservatively. Preserve existing seeds, ties, notes, visited identities and accepted geometry. Do not clear generated features wholesale: some have become real through play. Backfill knowledge from reliable recorded evidence and provide review for ambiguous cases; the absence of an old disclosure field cannot mean “nobody ever saw this.” Version the save contract and document the migration.

There is a modest library affordance here: wild maps already accept per-kind counts and explicit feature edits. However, features:{} still inherits the default feature counts, and changing counts or removing features can change cover and glyph placement. Suppressing the hut alone is neither a complete policy nor a safe blanket migration. Library changes must be made in C:\Projects\TnD_Maps and re-vendored with its pin, not patched into the vendored copy.

const hut = doc.features.find(f => f.id === 'hut-1');
console.log(hut);
console.log(Math.hypot(hut.x - 400, hut.y - 275) * doc.frame.mPerUnit);
