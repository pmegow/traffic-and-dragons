You are doing well at creating a distinctive game and insisting that its behavior match its promises. Your biggest management challenge is turning that growing capability into a smaller, reliably finished experience.

For Hannu · October 2, 2026 · Assessment of project leadership, delivery and game progress

My assessment: you are stronger as a product director and quality advocate than as a release manager. You notice what makes the game engaging, make concrete decisions, and challenge weak explanations. The release discipline needs a tighter definition of “enough,” fewer simultaneous commitments, and more validation that does not depend on you.

This is a judgment about the work I can observe, not your ability in the abstract. Some of the inefficiency belongs to the agents, including me: excessive verification time, mistaken assumptions, and implementations that need repeated correction are delivery failures we should own. You should not have to compensate for them by supervising every detail.

Keep doing these

Five things you are doing well

01

You have a clear sense of the experience.

The game has an identifiable purpose: an ongoing adventure whose people, possessions and history matter, including when played by voice. The Village extends that purpose by giving past characters a home. Portraits, readable cards and complete prose make authorship more tangible. These decisions connect features to something a player can feel.

In this conversation, you immediately recognized that portraits added value and asked to bring them to NPCs. You also supplied a concrete card layout. That is useful product direction: a visible example is easier to implement and judge than “make it nicer.”

Evidence: our Designer exchanges; creature and NPC portrait record; TODO #6 and #508.

02

You turn dissatisfaction into reproducible evidence.

You bring screenshots, exact text, device behavior and the circumstances of failure. You challenge an explanation when the visible result contradicts it. The clipped NPC description is a good example: the screenshot exposed an exact character-boundary failure, and your proposed full-draft/revision approach addressed the data loss.

Your rule to test the failure condition is one of the project's strongest practices. It makes “fixed” a claim that needs evidence.

Evidence: #547 diagnosis and verification; verification rules; #19 field reports.

03

You delegate real decisions while setting boundaries.

You chose per-creature portraits, authorized a sensible treasure taxonomy, and asked for feedback to be judged rather than accepted wholesale. On the stereo request, you repeatedly clarified the boundary: accurate software emulation, with no game-code changes. Those are useful distinctions between an outcome and the means of achieving it.

This combination gives an agent room to work without granting unlimited scope. Keep making explicit what success means and which decisions you want to retain.

Evidence: the requests and clarifications in this chat; accepted and declined panel recommendations.

04

You are willing to change the process when evidence warrants it.

The October 1 review recorded 12 defective entries among 46 reviewed probation entries, many involving silent state damage. You reinstated the stricter boundary for core work. That response matters: you treated a disappointing experiment as information and changed the operating rule.

The weekly review also examines actual play records and shipped diffs, rather than accepting builders' summaries as sufficient. This is a strong foundation for managing AI-assisted development.

Evidence: October 1 review and probation verdict; current core-change policy. This is a finding about that reviewed set, not a general model failure rate.

05

You noticed that quality control itself needed improvement.

Asking how many suites were running was the right management question. The parallel runner then addressed a measured bottleneck while retaining isolation and explicitly reporting failures that pass only when rerun alone.

The project record reports the earlier 72-battery sweep dropping from about 55 minutes to 13.5 minutes; the later ordering change ran a 73-battery range in 7.2 minutes. That is an improvement in feedback time with concrete evidence, rather than a blanket demand to test less.

Evidence: #548 measured results; shared runner. These are recorded runs, not a speed guarantee for every future sweep.

Change these habits

Five things to work on

01

Choose a release boundary and protect it.

The current project spans the core adventure, voice and car behavior, the Village, a substantial authoring tool, content libraries, and plans for remote multiplayer. Each has merit; together they create many ways for work to remain unfinished. Even the Designer discussion expanded naturally from models to creatures, treasure, portraits, layout and prose handling.

Recommendation: define one near-term release promise, such as “a new player can start, finish and resume a short adventure without intervention.” Limit active product work to three outcomes. An attractive new idea must replace one, or wait. If reliable in-car play is the next release promise instead, make it the primary acceptance gate and defer competing work.

Basis: breadth in #1, #6, #19 and #508. This is a prioritization recommendation, not a claim that these features should be abandoned.

02

Make human validation a regular part of delivery.

You are still the most visible source of real-use feedback in the records I examined. The creature panel is explicitly a review through sourced composite personas, not interviews or observed usability sessions. It can identify questions; it cannot establish that a newcomer understands the game or wants to return.

Recommendation: run a small, repeatable cohort of real players through the same short adventure. Observe the first session without coaching, then check whether they resume it unaided. Record where they stop, misunderstand the interface, or lose trust in the world. Your own expert play remains valuable, but it answers a different question.

Basis: the panel's stated method; reviewed play windows. I did not inspect a complete external-tester dataset.

03

Put spending and stopping rules around autonomous work.

The Thursday-work record says the follow-on unattended run consumed half the following week's Fable budget, much of it on build/review/rebuild loops that did not ship. Holding unsafe changes was correct. Letting that much work accumulate without a bounded decision point is a management problem worth addressing.

Recommendation: give each unattended assignment a budget, a concrete deliverable and a stop condition. After two unsuccessful repair/review cycles, require a short redesign assessment rather than another patch. Ask for cost and remaining uncertainty alongside completion status. “Keep going until done” needs a boundary when the architecture itself is in question.

Evidence: #448 follow-on run; held redesigns #534 and #545.

04

Judge quality by escaped failures and trust, not test totals.

The test infrastructure is substantial. It also missed consequential failures involving identity, abilities and inventory. The proof-gap record names behaviors claimed by commits that the tests did not actually enforce. More assertions alone will not close that gap, and assigning a stronger model does not make its work self-verifying.

Recommendation: retain independent review for core changes, but require the reviewer to exercise the real entry path and at least one failure the implementation's author did not choose. Track state corruption, lost progress and manual rescue separately from cosmetic defects. A report of 2,597 passing assertions should support an acceptance claim, never stand in for it.

Evidence: escaped defects; #528 proof gaps; this chat's final #547 test run.

05

Reduce the amount of closure that depends on you.

The tracker snapshot contains 40 rows marked ready for owner testing. Some bundle considerable work. That is a queue awaiting judgment, not 40 proven user outcomes. Long narrative rows preserve valuable history, but make it harder to see the few decisions that matter today. The weekly review also found recommendations that never became actionable rows.

Recommendation: hold one weekly acceptance session with at most five clearly described checks. Give every ready item an owner, a short pass/fail instruction and a date. Move historical detail into its linked record, while keeping the tracker authoritative. Delegate repeatable checks to testers; reserve your time for product judgments and real hardware behavior that only you can currently observe.

Evidence: October 2 tracker snapshot; unfiled follow-ups and owner checks. Counts describe rows, not comparable units of effort.

The game itself

Substantial progress; uneven readiness.

My stage assessment is an advanced, actively played development build suitable for a controlled beta. The records support a real game with persistent campaigns and substantial authoring and audio systems. They do not yet establish a dependable broad release. I would not assign a percentage complete: the destination is still expanding, and feature count conceals the remaining reliability work.

The most encouraging sign is that progress is visible in how the game can be played and authored, not merely in infrastructure. The most concerning sign is how often the system can report a plausible result while its stored state disagrees. For a persistent RPG, that damages the player's reason to care about choices. Reliable continuity deserves priority over another layer of breadth.

A practical next step

Make the next month about dependable play.

  1. Write one release promise. Choose the player, session length and supported platform. Name the features deliberately outside this release.
  2. Schedule a small real-player cohort. Five players is a useful starting point for finding friction, not statistical proof. Have them start, play, save and resume the same bounded experience.
  3. Close the highest-impact trust failures. Prioritize reproduced state loss and broken continuation paths. Require real-path evidence and review before declaring those outcomes safe.
  4. Bound agent work and clear the acceptance queue. Use cost limits and the two-cycle redesign checkpoint; accept a small batch of ready items each week.
  5. Review five outcome measures weekly. Unaided starts, successful save/resume, sessions needing rescue, voluntary returns, and cost/latency per completed session. Establish a baseline before setting targets; add route-specific audio results if car play is in scope.

The change I would most like to see: keep your ambition and your insistence on quality, but spend the next stretch finishing a narrower promise. You have built enough substance that learning how reliably other people can enjoy it is now more valuable than proving how many more things it can do.
