The assessment

The empty chairs and Mireille’s decade with an imitation of her husband are memorable, human-scale horror. The campaign has enough places to investigate and several people with reasons to falsify records. Its central risk is that the rules of invitation, the identities behind the altered names, and the price of refusing the bargain are not fixed enough to support a fair conclusion.

What to preserve

Keep grief as the monster’s access pointAn invitation made out of love is more affecting than a random undead attack. Mireille offers a difficult conversation with no need for combat.

Keep multiple compromised witnessesCorentin, Hesper and Nadault conceal different things. Their motives can complicate a fixed mystery without making every clue false.

Keep uncertainty about the Guest’s selfhoodWhether a being layered with memories believes itself to be the dead can remain unresolved. That ambiguity does not require the practical rules of stopping it to be arbitrary.

Improvements worth making

High: resolve before relying on the affected scene in a player-facing playtest. Medium: a concrete improvement to clarity, agency or payoff. These are editorial priorities, not measured gameplay failure rates.

  1. Put the Setting on one clear calendar
  2. Define the bargain’s due and the cost of refusing it
  3. Map the altered names to fixed authors and motives
  4. Decide how many bodies the Guest can inhabit and what invitation permits
  5. Fix Wick’s condition before offering a rescue
  6. Resolve the town’s chosen future rather than only the monster’s appearance
High priority · 1 of 6

Put the Setting on one clear calendar

Why this improves play

Act One appears to reach this year’s annual Setting, while Act Three is still preparing for it. The investigation can consequently acquire a second annual dinner, an unexplained year-long pause, or a deadline that rewinds to accommodate the plot.

Source evidence · 3 excerpts
acts[0].turningPoint“that night, an empty chair at the Setting is occupied”
acts[2].goal“before the coming Setting dinner”
locations[2].description“used only once a year for the autumn Setting dinner”
Recommended change

Make the Act One manifestation a household invitation or a clearly named rehearsal. Set the public annual Setting two nights later, or choose another explicit interval. State when the remaining investigation occurs and how the town responds to a requested delay.

Suggested wording or scene · proposal, not existing canon

Proposed timeline: arrival and house calls on the first day; an invited figure appears at the Ancoux supper that night. The official Setting in the town hall is two evenings away. Act Two investigates in that interval. Act Three begins when Nadault gathers the households for the single annual ceremony.

How to verify the improvement

Write the sequence on a three-day calendar. Every invitation, archive visit and dinner should fit without moving the deadline after the player acts.

High priority · 2 of 6

Define the bargain’s due and the cost of refusing it

Why this improves play

The town owes something, the bargain supplies protection, and names grant forms, but the amount due, the limits of that permission and the consequences of refusal remain undefined. A final choice between feeding, breaking or renegotiating the bargain cannot be meaningful if its effects are invented after the choice.

Source evidence · 4 excerpts
premise“a bargain that has kept something worse than death outside the harbor”
deepTime[0].note“to keep the harbor from flooding”
npcs[4].notes“a name given at the Setting is a name it may wear”
rules[1]“it can only be avoided, bargained with, or, at great cost, fed”
Recommended change

Create a short GM contract: what was originally promised, what counts as informed invitation, what Nadault has added, what the Guest can demand, and what happens when the annual exchange is refused. Keep the origin of the entity mysterious if desired; keep the observable consequences stable.

Suggested wording or scene · proposal, not existing canon

Proposed contract to consider: the original Compact permitted one voluntarily invited remembrance at the annual table in exchange for a year of calm tides. Nadault’s renewals added living identities to the due without telling the town. Refusing the false additions is possible if their fraud is exposed; ending the Compact restores ordinary storm danger rather than causing an unspecified extra monster to appear.

How to verify the improvement

Before the finale, the party should have enough evidence to explain the likely cost of three choices. The final bargain must not introduce a new compulsory sacrifice.

High priority · 3 of 6

Map the altered names to fixed authors and motives

Why this improves play

Four different sources can explain a changed entry, but the blueprint never assigns the important pages to their authors. The mysterious handwriting is also not attached to a named dead person. The GM may resolve the investigation by choosing whichever culprit seems convenient after hearing the player’s theory.

Source evidence · 4 excerpts
npcs[0].notes“he has been falsifying entries for years to keep her name off the Almanac”
npcs[3].notes“they altered one page themselves once, out of pity”
npcs[2].notes“each renewal requiring a fresh name added to the debt”
acts[0].turningPoint“written in the hand of someone who has been dead for eleven years”
Recommended change

Prepare a tiny evidence ledger for the author, not a long paperwork quest: one Corentin removal, one Hesper alteration, one Nadault addition, and one supernatural entry. Give each a distinct physical or witness clue and fix which deceased hand appears in the opening.

Suggested wording or scene · proposal, not existing canon

Proposed clue assignments: Corentin’s missing entry uses the same correction mark as his private ledger; Hesper’s compassionate change is an older overwritten date; Nadault’s added names coincide with his renewals; the fresh dead-hand entry appears on damp paper no living clerk handled. Record the exact people affected before play.

How to verify the improvement

Follow each suspicious page to its explanation without using the other pages. Two honest investigators should be able to disagree initially and still converge on the same facts.

High priority · 4 of 6

Decide how many bodies the Guest can inhabit and what invitation permits

Why this improves play

The prohibition on rising corpses conflicts with the guild’s description of repeatedly buried bodies. The singular Guest appears to support a long-term husband, Wick’s condition and a climactic arrival, but its simultaneous reach is not described. Players cannot reason about quarantine or withdrawal of invitation if its bodies and permissions change as needed.

Source evidence · 4 excerpts
rules[0]“The dead in Bellmere Hollow do not rise from graves”
acts[1].arcs[2].objective“bodies that don't stay buried”
npcs[1].notes“lived for a decade with something that looks and speaks like her drowned husband”
npcs[4].notes“an entity bound to Bellmere Hollow”
Recommended change

Define whether there is one active body, several limited echoes, or a shared presence with a fixed boundary. Explain the guild’s remains accordingly. State what opens a threshold, whether invitation can be withdrawn, and whether ordinary mention of a name counts.

Suggested wording or scene · proposal, not existing canon

Proposed rule: the Guest maintains invited echoes, not reanimated corpses. Destroyed echoes leave cold, inert remains; another appearance requires a fresh invitation and does not raise those remains. A deliberate invitation at a threshold or set place admits it; merely discussing a name does not. Withdrawing an invitation removes its right to that household, at a stated cost learned during investigation.

How to verify the improvement

Ask what happens when a household closes its door, revokes an invitation, destroys an echo or simply says a dead relative’s name. Use the same answers before and after the climax.

Medium priority · 5 of 6

Fix Wick’s condition before offering a rescue

Why this improves play

Leaving interpretation open is useful for atmosphere, but if the GM decides whether Wick is still himself only after the party acts, investigation cannot establish a reliable prognosis. The player’s attempt to save a child risks becoming a retrospective trick.

Source evidence · 1 excerpt
npcs[5].notes“Whether he is an early, incomplete Guest, a warning sign, or something the party can still save is left open for the table to discover.”
Recommended change

Choose Wick’s actual condition before the first diagnostic clue. His ultimate recovery can depend on player action; the starting facts should not. Include a non-sacrificial rescue route, its limits, and what evidence distinguishes Wick from a complete borrowed identity.

Suggested wording or scene · proposal, not existing canon

Proposed truth: Wick remains himself but carries an incomplete invitation made on his behalf. He remembers things he could not know, yet retains private memories the Guest cannot imitate. Releasing that invitation at the public table frees him; forcing him to offer another person’s name deepens the bond. Hesper knows one test that can reveal the difference.

How to verify the improvement

Investigate Wick early, then attempt the rescue late. Earlier clues must still mean what they appeared to mean, even if the rescue fails.

Medium priority · 6 of 6

Resolve the town’s chosen future rather than only the monster’s appearance

Why this improves play

Destroying an embodied guest does not automatically settle an indestructible fog or a continuing contract. Without a closing outcome, the player can win the confrontation and still have no answer about next year’s dinner, Nadault’s authority or the returned family members.

Source evidence · 3 excerpts
acts[2].turningPoint“expose, destroy, or strike a new bargain”
acts[2].arcs[1].objective“Confront the entity”
rules[1]“The fog beyond the Fogline cannot be dispelled”
Recommended change

Give each ending a short aftermath covering invitations, the harbor, Nadault, Mireille’s household and Wick. A costly win can preserve the fog while ending coerced invitations. A failed renegotiation should leave an explicit continuing burden, not reset the same mystery indefinitely.

Suggested wording or scene · proposal, not existing canon

Proposed epilogue after ending the fraudulent renewals: the fog remains offshore, but the town’s doors are its own again. Nadault loses the borrowed years that maintained his office; the people choose whether to keep a remembrance dinner without inviting the Guest. Mireille must decide how to mourn honestly. Wick’s state follows what the party actually accomplished.

How to verify the improvement

After each ending, answer “What happens at the next Setting?” without inventing a new term of the bargain.

Catalog description

Published description at review time

Horror and mystery. A fog-locked harbor town lays extra chairs for its dead every autumn. You arrive to take the census and learn the ledger is a contract, and the names on it are changing.

Keep the existing pitch. It gives the player the campaign’s contract mystery while reserving the Guest’s identity and the human betrayals. The empty chairs do more work than an additional adjective would.

The first revision pass

Set the calendar and contract first. Assign the clue authors and Wick’s condition next. Then test an investigation where the Almanac is damaged and nobody accepts a new invitation.
